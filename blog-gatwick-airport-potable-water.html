What is emergency potable water?

Emergency potable water is drinking-quality water supplied temporarily when a normal water supply is unavailable, inadequate or interrupted. At a large site such as an airport, emergency potable water can be delivered by tanker and transferred into suitable receiving storage or into an agreed site arrangement, depending on the circumstances.

The key word is potable. Water intended for drinking, cooking and other human-consumption purposes is subject to specific requirements. Emergency supply should therefore be planned around an appropriate potable water source and suitable delivery arrangements rather than simply treating any bulk water tanker as interchangeable.

The Drinking Water Inspectorate recognises tanker supply as one of the possible emergency arrangements where normal drinking-water supplies are disrupted. citeturn0search2

Gatwick water tanker delivery: what needs to be considered?

Arranging a tanker delivery to an airport is different from arranging a delivery to an ordinary construction site. The physical delivery is only one part of the job. The site, access arrangements, receiving point, storage capacity and timing all need to be considered before the vehicle arrives.

1. Exact delivery location

“Gatwick Airport” covers a large and complex operational environment. A tanker driver needs a clear delivery location rather than simply a general airport name. The relevant terminal, building, service area, contractor compound or operational site should be identified as accurately as possible.

If the requirement is for a site close to Gatwick rather than the airport itself, provide the full postcode and site address. This allows the delivery requirement to be assessed properly.

2. Airport access and security

Airport deliveries can involve controlled access, security procedures, designated routes, timed deliveries and site-specific rules. These requirements can affect how a tanker reaches the receiving point.

For that reason, emergency water enquiries should include any information already known about access restrictions. If a delivery needs to reach an airside, restricted or controlled area, the site's own authorised contact should confirm the relevant access procedure and requirements.

3. How much potable water is required?

The quantity required depends on what the water is being used for and how long the interruption is expected to last. A short interruption affecting a limited facility may require a very different volume from a prolonged outage affecting a major operational area.

Useful information includes the number of people affected, the facilities that have lost supply, available storage capacity, expected duration and whether the water is needed for drinking and domestic use, catering, cleaning, welfare, aircraft-related requirements or another approved purpose.

4. Where can the tanker discharge?

Before a tanker arrives, the receiving point should be understood. Is there a suitable storage tank? Is there a connection point? How far is the receiving point from the vehicle? Is a hose route available? Are there restrictions on where the tanker can park?

These details matter because a tanker delivery is not simply a matter of parking and opening a valve. The transfer arrangement needs to be practical and suitable for the site.

5. How long will the water be needed?

If the outage is expected to last for several hours, a single emergency delivery may be sufficient depending on storage and consumption. If the outage continues for a longer period, repeat tanker deliveries may need to be planned.

Planning repeat deliveries can be particularly important for high-footfall sites. Waiting until storage is nearly empty before arranging the next delivery can create unnecessary pressure on an already disrupted operation.

What to do if your Gatwick site has no water

If your site has suddenly lost its water supply, start by confirming the extent of the issue and contacting the relevant water supplier or site facilities team. If an emergency temporary supply is required, gather the information needed for a tanker enquiry as quickly as possible.

  1. Confirm the site location and postcode.
  2. Confirm which facilities are affected.
  3. Estimate how much potable water is required.
  4. Check whether there is suitable storage available.
  5. Identify the tanker access and discharge point.
  6. Confirm any airport security or site access requirements.
  7. Confirm when the water is required.
  8. Consider whether further deliveries may be needed.

Providing these details at the beginning can help the tanker supplier understand the requirement and identify the most practical delivery arrangement.
